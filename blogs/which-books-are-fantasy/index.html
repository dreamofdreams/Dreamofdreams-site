What Makes a Book Fantasy?

Ask ten readers which books are fantasy and you will get ten different lists. That is not a failure of the genre. It is the genre's defining feature. Fantasy is a broad category of imaginative fiction in which the story depends on elements that do not exist in the real world: magic, invented worlds, mythical creatures, or supernatural forces. A book is fantasy when removing those elements would collapse the plot, not merely decorate it.

That test separates fantasy from fiction that simply feels strange. A novel about a grieving family with one odd neighbor is not fantasy, even if the neighbor is eccentric. A novel where that neighbor turns out to be an angel guarding a doorway between worlds is. The extraordinary element has to carry weight.

Here at Dream Of Dreams, we know how much a reader can be drawn into an invented world. The question "which books are fantasy?" is really a question about what a reader is hungry for. Some want escape. Some want meaning. Most want both.

The Line Between Fantasy and Science Fiction

The usual dividing line is explanation. Fantasy leaves the impossible unexplained or explains it through magic, myth, or the divine. Science fiction explains it through technology, physics, or biology, however speculative.

The line blurs constantly. A story about a portal opened by a machine is science fiction. The same portal opened by a prayer is fantasy. Many readers simply call both speculative fiction and move on. That is a perfectly reasonable position.

The Main Subgenres of Fantasy Explained

Subgenres exist to help you find your next read faster, not to trap books in boxes. Most fantasy novels borrow from two or three at once. Use these as signposts, not fences.

High Fantasy, Epic Fantasy, and Portal Fantasy

High fantasy takes place entirely in a secondary world with its own rules, geography, and history. Epic fantasy is high fantasy at scale: long arcs, large casts, and stakes that decide the fate of a world. Portal fantasy begins in our world and crosses over into another.

Dark Fantasy, Fairy Tales, and Magical Realism

Dark fantasy keeps the magic and adds dread. Fairy tales are the genre's oldest root, and modern retellings still shape what readers expect. Magical realism treats the impossible as ordinary and unremarkable, which makes it feel closer to literary fiction than to quests and battles.

Before the list, here is the criteria we used, so you can judge our choices and make your own. A book earns a place here if it (1) depends on its impossible elements rather than decorating with them, (2) is widely available and widely read enough that a new reader can find it easily, (3) represents a distinct subgenre so the list teaches you the shape of the genre, and (4) is a reasonable entry point, not the deepest, densest volume in a long series.

  • The Hobbit by J.R.R. Tolkien, high fantasy, gentle and warm, a natural doorway into epic fantasy. A single volume, suitable for readers of nearly any age, with mild peril and no graphic violence.
  • A Wizard of Earthsea by Ursula K. Le Guin, high fantasy, short, wise, and quietly profound. A slim single volume that rewards slow reading; a good choice if you want meaning more than spectacle.
  • The Name of the Wind by Patrick Rothfuss, epic fantasy, lyrical and immersive. The first book of an unfinished series; beautiful prose, but be aware you are beginning a longer commitment.
  • The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe by C.S. Lewis, portal fantasy, allegory woven into adventure. Short, accessible, and a classic entry point for readers who want wonder without darkness.
  • The Alchemist by Paulo Coelho, fable-like and often shelved near fantasy. A short single volume; some readers argue it is not fantasy at all, and we would say it belongs on the shelf anyway, because it does what the best fantasy does: it uses an impossible journey to say something true about an ordinary life.
  • The Lies of Locke Lamora by Scott Lynch, dark fantasy and heist adventure, set in a vivid invented city. A single volume that opens a series; sharper edges, wit, and violence, so choose it when you want peril rather than comfort.
  • Uprooted by Naomi Novik, fairy-tale fantasy rooted in folklore. A single volume with a slow-burning relationship at its heart; a good bridge for readers who want romance and atmosphere alongside magic.
  • The House in the Cerulean Sea by TJ Klune, cozy fantasy, warm and character-driven. A single volume, low on violence, high on found family; a gentle starting point for readers in a hard season.

A few patterns are worth noticing. The shorter books on this list, Earthsea, The Hobbit, The Alchemist, are the safest first steps because they ask the least of your time. The longer or darker books reward a reader who already knows they enjoy the genre. And the cozy and fairy-tale entries show that fantasy is not one mood; it can be comfort or dread, and both are legitimate.

That last point matters if you are reading through a difficult time. Some readers need a world that soothes; others need a world that mirrors the struggle and then shows a way through. Neither choice is wrong. Our list of comforting fantasy reads for difficult times is a good place to begin if you want the gentler door.

Which Books Are Fantasy Romance?

Fantasy romance is any fantasy novel where the romantic relationship drives the plot rather than decorating it. Remove the love story and the book falls apart. That is the test, and it is the same test we applied to fantasy itself.

The subgenre has grown enormously, and it now spans everything from court intrigue to quiet village romances. Readers who want the relationship to matter without the world ending will find plenty to love.

The Best Fantasy Book Series to Commit To

A series asks for something a single novel never does: your time, your patience, and your willingness to be changed slowly. The best fantasy book series earn that commitment through consistency of vision, not just page count.

Look for three things before you commit:

  • A first book that stands on its own, even if it opens a larger door
  • A world that rewards attention rather than requiring a wiki
  • A cast whose relationships deepen instead of resetting

Here is where most recommendation lists stop, and where we want to go further. Instead of ranking series by popularity, it helps to sort them by the kind of commitment they ask of you.

The classic long commitment. J.R.R. Tolkien's The Lord of the Rings is the benchmark: three volumes, a deep invented history, and a slow build that rewards patience.

The doorstop epic. George R.R. Martin's A Song of Ice and Fire and Robert Jordan's The Wheel of Time offer enormous casts and decades-spanning arcs.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are considered fantasy books?

Fantasy books are stories set in worlds or situations that could not exist in our own, usually because they include magic, mythical creatures, or invented geography. A novel counts as fantasy when the impossible is treated as real within the story's own rules. That covers everything from high fantasy epics with quests and prophecies to quieter tales where magic brushes against an ordinary town. If the story asks you to accept something beyond the natural world, you are almost certainly reading fantasy.

What makes a book fantasy rather than science fiction?

The usual dividing line is explanation. Science fiction tends to explain its wonders through technology, science, or future history, while fantasy presents magic and the supernatural as given parts of the world. A starship run by physics is science fiction; a kingdom held together by prophecy is fantasy. Plenty of books blend the two, so treat the line as a helpful guide rather than a wall. Ask what the story wants you to accept on faith, and you will usually find your answer.

Are fantasy books only about magic?

No. Magic is a common tool, but the genre's real subject is often courage, loyalty, grief, or the cost of power. Some fantasy novels contain almost no spellcasting and still belong firmly to the genre because their world is invented and their stakes are mythic. Many readers come for the magical world and stay for the characters. If you enjoy stories about people facing impossible choices, fantasy has more to offer you than wizards and dragons alone.
